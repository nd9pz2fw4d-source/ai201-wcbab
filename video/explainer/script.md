# Follow the claim: before the session

A pre-read for the Executive AI Academy, in three voices: about 8 minutes with two narrators, then about 3 minutes where the session's host introduces the day (11½ minutes in all). It goes out with a one-page cheat sheet. No song.

The session is about WCB: the claim, our choices, our accountabilities. The narrators' part is about you and the world: the AI you already meet, what it looks like when it works and when it slips, how to see through a pitch, and the questions to bring. It does not walk through the session's slides. The host's part is the trailer for the day itself.

**Ava** and **Sam**: two co-narrators (a woman and a man) who trade lines like colleagues from down the hall: witty, warm and steady, with a light Alberta touch (construction season, the forecast, the block heater). Fun, never silly; every joke sits next to a fact. Neither is a real person. **Host:** Riphay Al-Hussein, who leads the session, in their own (cloned) voice; comes in at the end to introduce the day: welcoming, clear, a little brisk.

Pronunciation: the voice is sent "Rifai" wherever the script says "Riphay" (scripts/explainer.mjs); captions keep "Riphay".

Pace: the voices' natural speed (about 155 words a minute), with a short pause between lines and between scenes. "(pause 2.5s)" after a line holds the picture for the viewer to think before the answer. Every spoken line is captioned. Lines marked **Ava:**, **Sam:** or **Host:** are spoken; words in [square brackets] direct the delivery and are neither spoken nor captioned. Lines starting "On screen" describe the picture and are not spoken. The three demos are made-up examples, labelled "Illustrative" on screen.

## 0. Opening card

On screen: the "Follow the claim" title card, with a whoosh and a stamp. About 4 seconds, then Ava starts.

## 1. Why watch

On screen: a packed calendar; then the four parts of the video as chapter cards.

**Ava:** [warmly] Hi there, and welcome. I know your calendar is busier than the QE2 on a long weekend, so let's make these ten minutes count.

**Sam:** The session will use a handful of words about AI. And here's the good news: you already know more about them than you think. You've been using AI since breakfast.

**Ava:** So, here's the plan. First, a day with AI. Then we'll watch it work, and watch it slip. Then, how to see through a sales pitch. And finally, three questions to bring with you. There's no quiz at the end. [short pause] Probably.

## 2. A Tuesday with AI: the next word

On screen: a phone keyboard. "Running late, be there in" is typed; the suggestions "10", "five", "a minute" appear. Then the label "Large language model (LLM)".

**Sam:** Seven thirty, Tuesday morning. You're texting your team that you're running late, because it's construction season, which in Alberta is the other season. And your phone finishes the sentence for you.

**Ava:** That's the core idea behind tools like Copilot. A large language model, or LLM, predicts a likely next word, again and again. Your keyboard does a tiny version of it.

**Sam:** So, basically, a very, very good autocomplete, trained on a mountain of text.

**Ava:** That's why these tools write so fluently. And it's also why they can be wrong: they produce what sounds likely, not what they've checked. On its own, it has no fact check, and no access to our files.

## 3. A Tuesday with AI: the bank alert

On screen: a text from the bank, "Did you just spend $1,240 in another city? Reply YES or NO". Then the label "Predictive AI (machine learning)".

**Sam:** Eight fifteen. Your bank texts you: did you just spend twelve hundred dollars in another city? [short pause] You did not. You've been in traffic for forty minutes.

**Ava:** That's a different kind of AI. Predictive AI, or machine learning, learns patterns from past data and scores how unusual something is. A bit like an Alberta weather forecast: a confident call, based on patterns. Very useful. Still, bring a jacket. WCB already uses this kind, to forecast and to sort.

**Sam:** And notice what the bank does next. It doesn't cancel your card for good. It asks you. When the stakes are high, the machine flags, and a person decides.

## 4. A Tuesday with AI: "page four"

On screen: a colleague's chat reply, "Yes, section 3.2 says so. Page 4 of the policy," with a link chip. Then the labels "Grounding" and "RAG: retrieval-augmented generation".

**Ava:** Ten o'clock. You ask a colleague about a policy, and she says: yes, it's on page four.

**Sam:** And you trust it, because she showed you where. That's grounding. A grounded AI looks things up in trusted sources first, answers from them, and shows you the source.

**Ava:** If she'd just said, "I think so," you'd check. Hold AI to the same standard.

**Sam:** You'll also hear grounding called RAG: retrieval-augmented generation. [short pause] Yes, a mouthful. Retrieve the right documents, add them to the question, then generate the answer from them. And it's only as good as the documents we give it.

## 5. A Tuesday with AI: the trip

On screen: two panels. Left, a chatbot listing flights. Right, an agent working through steps (flight, hotel, calendar), then pausing on "Pay $1,860?" with Approve and Decline buttons.

**Sam:** One o'clock. You need to be in Calgary on Thursday.

**Ava:** A chatbot would list some flights. An agent books the trip. It has a goal, tools it's allowed to use, some memory of your preferences, and instructions. It plans a step, acts, checks the result, and repeats. Picture a very keen new hire who never needs a coffee break.

**Sam:** It can only use what it's connected to: it can search flights and hold a hotel, but it can't spend from the company account. Only the access the job needs. That's called least privilege.

**Ava:** And a good one stops before it pays, and asks you. That's the autonomy dial: how much it may do alone, from just suggesting, to drafting, to acting with approval, all the way to acting alone. You, approving, are the human in the loop.

**Sam:** So that's three kinds of AI. One predicts. One writes. One does.

## 6. Watch it work: two summaries

On screen: an AI assistant window, labelled "Illustrative". The prompt "Summarize this 40-message thread". Two answers side by side: one with source chips under each point, one without. A countdown, then a tick on the sourced answer.

**Ava:** Now let's watch some AI at work. Here are two summaries of the same long email thread. Which would you trust?

(pause 2.5s)

**Sam:** If you picked the one that shows where each point came from, good call. Both read well. Only one lets you check it in seconds.

**Ava:** Now picture a thick medical file instead of an email thread. A summary is only useful if the person deciding can see the page it came from.

## 7. Watch it slip: the confident answer

On screen: the assistant is asked "What did the leadership team decide last Tuesday?" A beat with "What do you think happens?" Then a confident, specific answer. Then a stamp: "Made up".

**Sam:** Next, we ask it something it can't know: what the leadership team decided last Tuesday. It wasn't in the room, and nobody gave it the notes. What do you think happens?

(pause 2.5s)

**Ava:** You might expect it to say, "I don't know." Sometimes it does.

**Sam:** Here, it gives a confident, specific answer, and none of it happened. That's called a hallucination. Think of a fish story from the lake, told with total confidence, and a budget attached.

**Ava:** Why would it do that? Remember the next word. It's built to produce a likely-sounding answer, not to say "I wasn't there."

**Sam:** The fix is the same as before: ground it in the right sources, ask where an answer came from, and have a person check anything that matters. Fluent is not the same as verified.

## 8. Watch it work: the draft

On screen: the assistant drafts a reply to a worker who is upset about a delay. A person's cursor changes one sentence to warmer wording, then clicks Send. Labels: "AI drafts", "A person decides", "Who sent it? The person".

**Ava:** Last one. It drafts a reply to a worker who's upset about a delay. It's quick and mostly right. But a person reads it, changes one sentence to sound more human, and only then sends it.

**Sam:** So, if that reply had gone out wrong, whose name is on it?

**Ava:** The person who sent it owns it. AI can draft, sort and fetch, but accountability doesn't move to the machine. That's why, for anything that reaches a worker or decides their benefits, a person stays in the loop.

## 9. Decode the pitch

On screen: a glossy pitch slide: "Our AI agent is 99% accurate, fully autonomous, learns your business, and plugs into all your systems." Each phrase is struck through in turn and replaced by a question, numbered 1 to 5.

**Sam:** Now, a pitch you may have heard: "Our AI agent is ninety-nine percent accurate, fully autonomous, learns your business, and plugs into all your systems." [short pause] Sounds great. Let's decode it.

**Ava:** "Ninety-nine percent accurate": on what tests, and on cases like ours? Those tests are called evals. Ask to see them.

**Sam:** "Fully autonomous": for which tasks, and where does a person approve? "Learns your business": from what data, and can it show where an answer came from?

**Ava:** "Plugs into all your systems": what can it read, what can it draft, and what can it actually do? Around here, the only thing that should plug into everything is a block heater. Only the access the job needs: least privilege.

**Sam:** And one more they rarely mention: when it's wrong, who notices, and can we see what it did?

**Ava:** If they can't answer those, [short pause] that is your answer.

**Sam:** Five questions, ready for the next time a vendor calls. They're on your cheat sheet, too.

## 10. Three questions to bring

On screen: one line from Deloitte's 2026 paper, with its source. Then three question cards, one at a time. Then the cheat sheet, sliding in.

**Ava:** One last thing. In Deloitte's 2026 survey of Canadian workers' compensation leaders, eighty-three percent said their AI maturity is still low. So nobody's far ahead. What matters is where we choose to start.

**Sam:** Before the session, think about your own area. First: where does work wait the longest? Waiting is where AI usually helps first.

**Ava:** Second: which decision would you never hand to a machine, and why? Your answer says a lot about where a person must stay.

**Sam:** And third: what would you need to see before you trusted an AI's answer? A source, a test result, a person who checked?

**Ava:** The words are all on the one-page cheat sheet that came with this video. Bring it, and bring your answers. [warmly] And now, we'll hand you over to Riphay, who's leading the session, for a quick look at the day itself.

**Host:** The autonomy dial: how much it does alone is a choice we make. And evals: test before you trust, with a human in the loop.
